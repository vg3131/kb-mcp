## Aggregate and Gloss: Why Some Floors Won't Pop

The combination of aggregate type, mix design, and the polishing process determines whether a floor achieves a crisp, high-clarity result. Concrete with a high proportion of manufactured sand, recycled aggregate, or dark/grey basalt aggregate can look dull at 400+ grit even with perfect technique. The aggregate itself doesn't polish to the same reflectivity as quartz or crystolic stone. Set expectations at quoting stage — and run a test patch to confirm achievable gloss.

Cold joints from delayed pours create visible colour, texture and aggregate inconsistencies. They're structural — polishing can't fix them. Nothing is documented on pour day, so polishers discover them too late.

## Crack Filling vs Crack Repairing: Never Use the Word Repair

In the polished concrete industry, you never 'repair' a crack — you 'fill' it. The reason is legal and practical: if you call it a repair, you own it. If it cracks again (which it might, since shrinkage continues for 18 months), you are liable. Fill it, document it, and move on. Crack types vary: surface crazing, structural shrinkage, movement joints. Each requires a different approach.

> If you use the word repair, you own it. We fill cracks. Always. If it cracks again — and it might — that's not your repair failing.

## Edges Tell the Whole Story

Hand-trowelled edges show lighter colour, chatter marks, and pushed-down aggregate. If edges look bad, every mid-floor defect gets scrutinised. Get the edges right first — the rest becomes forgivable.

> If the edges are bad, every defect gets looked at. If the edges are good, no one looks in the middle.

## Efflorescence: Why It Happens and How to Stop It

Efflorescence (white mineral deposits) occurs when water moves through concrete and evaporates, depositing calcium carbonate. It's not a polished concrete failure — it's a water movement problem. Prevention: ensure the slab is properly moisture-cured before grinding, use a densifier to close pores, and protect finished floors from water infiltration. Once it appears, light grinding or specific acid treatment removes it.

> Polished concrete is meant to be indestructible — so why is it happening? It's not a product failure. It's water movement. Any porous building material can effloresce.

Source: TechTalk: Efflorescence


## Fibre Reinforced Concrete: What It Means for Polishing

Fibre reinforced concrete (steel or polypropylene fibres) is increasingly common in industrial slabs. Steel fibres can appear at the surface during grinding — they're usually manageable. Polypropylene fibres melt during trowelling and create a microporosity that affects densifier absorption. Identify whether fibres are present before quoting and factor in additional densifier application.

> Fibres are becoming more common. Steel fibres at the surface are manageable — but polypropylene fibres change how the surface absorbs everything you put on it.

## Manufactured Sands Pop Out Late

Natural sands (spherical, smooth) pop out during early grinding. Crusher dust (wedge-shaped, porous) bonds more strongly and doesn't release until polishing stages (100–400 grit) — causing scratches and aggregate pop-outs. Keep crusher dust below 30% of sand fraction.

> You're working harder to get crusher dust out — hence seeing it in the later stages.

## Oils in Floors: You Must Identify Before Quoting

## Overlays: When Existing Concrete Is Too Bad to Polish

## Residential Slabs Are Designed to Crack

Beams prevent heave but restrain shrinkage — so the slab cracks instead. More top reinforcement keeps cracks tight but costs $4–5/sqm extra. Shrinkage takes 18 months to settle in industrial; in residential, polishing before that means cracks may reopen.

> Residential slabs are made for cracking. The beams cause restraint — we can't stop the concrete from shrinking.

## Topping Slabs: The Better Choice for Polish

80–100mm thick, no attached beams, poured after walls are up. Only 3–4 truck loads, controlled environment, can align joints with re-entering corners. Hydronic heating makes the upsell easy. Far more predictable than polishing a structural raft slab.

> Topping slabs are a lot more reliable. If they don't work, it's not a bomb to replace part of it.

## Is the Slab Ready to Grind? No Simple Answer

Time-based rules (e.g. 'grind after 28 days') ignore the real variables: environment (inside vs outside), temperature, slump, whether it's on-ground or suspended, and slab thickness. The best test is physical: scratch test with a nail for hardness, and check moisture with plastic sheeting taped for 24 hours. If moisture beads under the sheet, it's too wet for a sealer — but may still be grindable.

> Is it ready to grind after five days? I don't know. It depends on so many factors — environment, inside or out, hot day, cold day, slump, on ground.

## Keep the Floor Wet: 10–15 Minutes First Coat

After the first application, keep the surface wet for 10–15 minutes. Water carriers help draw silicate in via capillary action. If it flashes off, surface pores seal and subsequent coats can't penetrate.

> Get it down heavy first and keep it wet for 10–15 minutes — that's the concrete getting the opportunity to draw the product in.

## Lick Coats Gone Wrong

Over-applying densifier at the wrong stage creates a crystalline layer that tools can't cut through. If densifier sits too long without being worked in, it dehydrates on the surface creating 'concrete acne.' The fix usually requires going back to metals — which means rework. Prevention: control application rates and timing.

> Concrete acne — never looks attractive. Getting it off without damaging the profile is the real challenge.

## Modified Concrete: How Additives Change Your Process

Concrete plants are increasingly adding polymers, fibres, shrinkage reducers, and other admixtures. These modify porosity, surface hardness and how densifier absorbs. A slab that looks normal can absorb differently because of invisible additives — densifier sits on top instead of penetrating. If you start going backwards at 50 resin, go back to check densifier absorption before blaming the tool.

> Modern concrete has a lot of juice in it from the plants. It affects us contractors right through the polishing stage — changes process, changes everything.

Source: TechTalk: Dealing with Modified Concrete · Dealing With New Types Of Concrete · 0:53


## Multiple Applications: Why Each Grind Step Gets More

Each grind step (50, 100, 200) opens new pores and removes barriers like polymer residue from grouting. Applying densifier after each step gets silicate into fresh surface. Resin tools generate heat that catalyses the reaction. Every step is diminishing returns but still measurable.

> By running resins through moist densifier, we're catalyzing the reaction with temperature.

Source: Ep 6: Densifiers · Tim · 49:18


## Point of Saturation: What It Means

You've reached saturation when excess densifier builds up on the surface. Without this buildup, you can't confirm the concrete has received maximum silicate. The concrete's Part B (free lime) reacts with the densifier's Part A (silicate) to form calcium silicate hydrate — a hard crystalline structure.

> Unless you're getting buildup on the surface, you don't know you've got enough in.

Source: Ep 6: Densifiers · Tim · 7:53


## Potassium Early, Lithium Late

Potassium silicate is heavier — apply at 50–200 grit when the floor is porous. Lithium has a smaller molecule and penetrates closed surfaces — use at 400 grit and beyond. Introducing lithium too early wastes the product because the floor hasn't closed enough.

> Run potassium to 200s — you'll see the difference between matrix and stone.

Source: Ep 9: Kurt Scammell · 1:09:18


## Solids Content: Why 40%+ Matters

A 15% solids product puts mostly water into porous concrete — silicate spreads deep and thin, away from the polishing surface. A 40%+ product stays concentrated near the top 5mm where free lime is, creating a denser reaction zone exactly where you need it.

> High-concentration product at the surface reacts faster with available free lime — double advantage.

Source: Ep 6: Densifiers · Tim · 35:27

When contractors say 'very hard concrete', it often means the surface is impermeable — not that the aggregate is hard. A burnished trowel finish on 32 MPA concrete can feel harder than 50 MPA that was wet-finished. The fix for impermeable surfaces is different to the fix for high-MPA aggregate. Diagnose which problem you have before changing tooling.

> Hard concrete — I'm not necessarily convinced. There are so many factors. The surface treatment can make 32 MPA feel harder than 50 MPA.

# Glossary


## CSP — Concrete Surface Profile

CSP (Concrete Surface Profile) is the standardised measurement of surface texture/roughness after preparation. CSP 1–3 is suitable for thin coatings and penetrating sealers. CSP 3–5 suits epoxy and thicker coatings. CSP 6–9 is used for trowelled-on overlays. Mechanical grinding typically produces CSP 2–4 depending on diamond grit. Know the required CSP before specifying a finish.

> CSP is the language that connects surface preparation to coating adhesion. If a coating fails, CSP is the first thing to verify.

Source: TechTalk: Glossary


## GFRC / GRC — Glass Fibre Reinforced Concrete

GFRC (Glass Fibre Reinforced Concrete) is a mix incorporating alkali-resistant glass fibres — used in precast panels and decorative elements. It's much thinner than structural concrete. Polishing GFRC requires very light tool pressure, minimal water, and an awareness that you're working with less material. Any aggressive grinding risks cutting through to the backing layer.

> GFRC is thin — you're often only working with 10–15mm of face concrete. Treat it like surgery.

Source: TechTalk: Materials Talk · 2:59


## MPA — Megapascal and Concrete Strength

MPA (megapascal) is the measure of concrete compressive strength. Standard residential slab: 20–25 MPA. Standard commercial: 32 MPA. High performance: 40–50 MPA. Higher MPA means denser concrete with less free lime and less porosity. This affects densifier absorption rate, tool bond selection, and how fast the floor responds to each polishing stage.

> Higher MPA, denser concrete, less free lime. Your densifier process needs to compensate — more applications, heavier concentration.

Source: Ep 6: Densifiers · Ep 10: Peter Killeen · 8:24


## Metal Bond, Resin Bond and Hybrid Tools

Metal bond diamonds are sintered — diamond segments suspended in a metal matrix. Used for aggressive cutting, flattening and early-stage grinding. Resin bond diamonds use a polymer matrix — finer cut, higher clarity, used for honing and polishing. Hybrid tools combine both matrices. Bond hardness (soft to super-hard) is matched to the concrete hardness inversely — hard concrete needs a soft bond to expose fresh diamond.

> Soft concrete needs a hard bond, hard concrete needs a soft bond. Getting that wrong is the single biggest tool-selection mistake.

Source: TechTalk: Bonds Combos · Ep 4


## P-Rating — Slip Resistance Classification

P-ratings (AS 4586) classify slip resistance of floor surfaces tested wet. P1: low risk (dry-only areas). P2: low risk (covered areas). P3: moderate risk (covered external, car parks). P4: high risk (external areas exposed to weather). P5: highest risk (pool surrounds, fish markets). Mechanically polished concrete at 400 grit typically achieves P3–P4 wet. Burnished finishes are lower. Always test and document.

> Know your P-rating. If someone slips and you haven't documented it, that falls on you.

Source: TechTalk: Slip Testing · Gary from Slip Testing Australia · 17:40

## Adjusting Tools When Performance Drops Mid-Job

## Aggregate Popping Out at Resin Stage

Late-stage aggregate pop-out during resin work (100–400 grit) is almost always manufactured sand (crusher dust) releasing after the matrix bond is broken down by resin heat. Crusher dust particles are wedge-shaped and bond strongly to cement paste — they survive metal bonds but release under resin friction. If this is happening, reduce RPM and run wetter at the 100–200 grit stage.

> Crusher dust — it's more porous, more surface bonding. You're working harder to get it out at the later stages.

A donut pattern (darker ring with lighter centre) appears when the machine runs too slowly in one spot, creating more heat and cutting in the centre and less at the outer edge. It can also appear when densifier pools under the machine during application. Keep the machine moving at consistent pace. Apply densifier ahead of the machine, not under it.

## Sealer Application: Temperature and Humidity Rules

Totally Defend and other solvent-based impregnating sealers require: ambient and surface temperature between 15–25°C, low relative humidity (below 75–80% RH), and product temperature matching ambient. In cool climates, solvents separate from solids inside the tin — the sealer looks normal but won't penetrate. Fix: warm the tin in the sun or a bucket of warm water before opening. Never apply cold sealer. In humid conditions, the solvent carrier cannot evaporate — sealer stays wet on the surface and blooms.

> Solvents separate from solids in cool climates. Put the sealer in the sun or a bucket of warm water to warm it up and reactivate the molecules before use.

Source: Polishing Process Guide & Sealer Failure Doc · Worx+ Technical Docs


## Sealer Blooming: Causes and Recognition

Blooming (also called sealer failure) occurs when a solvent-based impregnating sealer is applied in suboptimal conditions — typically substrate moisture above acceptable levels, ambient humidity too high, or product temperature too cold causing solvent-solid separation. It manifests as a sticky film, white haze, or efflorescence-like crystallisation on the surface. The sealer has cured on the surface rather than inside the concrete, blocking breathability.

> This type of failure is often referred to as blooming — uncured sealer manifesting as a sticky film or efflorescence-like crystallisation. The substrate must breathe again before reinstating.

Source: Surface Polish Sealer Failure: Restoration Methods · Worx+ Technical Doc


## Sealer Failure Method 1: Solvent-Assisted Surface Breakdown

For fresh or minor blooming: (1) Ventilate area, ensure 15–25°C, address any moisture. (2) Test a small inconspicuous patch first. (3) Dry mop surface. (4) Apply a light even coat of the same solvent-based sealer via pressure sprayer — the solvents in the fresh sealer reactivate and soften the failed layer. Allow 5–10 minutes dwell time, mop side-to-side repeatedly. (5) After 30 minutes, remove excess using a planetary machine with XD Burnishing Pad or HD Polishing Pad at one grit higher than the original finish. (6) Dry for 1 hour, inspect. If clean — apply final light coat per manufacturer instructions. If not — proceed to Method 2.

> Apply fresh sealer to reactivate the failed layer — the solvents soften and re-emulsify the bloom. One grit higher than the original finish removes the residue.

Source: Surface Polish Sealer Failure: Restoration Methods · Worx+ Technical Doc


## Sealer Failure Method 2: Full Mechanical Reinstatement

For persistent blooming where Method 1 fails: (1) Ventilate, 15–25°C, fix moisture. (2) Test 3–10 sqm first. (3) Dry mop, then apply Boost Potassium via E-Sprayer. Hone with 50-grit Specialty Pro or Impact while surface is damp. (4) Apply wet coat of Boost Potassium, mist ahead of grinder. Hone with 100-grit Universal or Impact while damp. (5) Apply Boost Lithium — E-Sprayer, even coverage, no microfibre at this stage. Allow to dry fully. (6) Mist Boost Lithium ahead of machine, hone with 200-grit Universal or Impact while damp. (7) Apply second coat Boost Lithium (microfibre OK here), dry fully. (8) Use 400-grit Flex or Tempo XD 400 dry until 100% surface refinement. Finish to satin (200 Tempo HD pads) or gloss (1500 Flex/XD 1500) then reseal.

> It is imperative that all uncured sealer deposits are fully removed to restore breathability before reinstating the impregnating sealer.

Source: Surface Polish Sealer Failure: Restoration Methods · Worx+ Technical Doc


## Sealer Streaking or White Haze

White haze or streaking after applying Totally Defend is almost always one of: slab or product too cold (below 10°C), applied too thickly, excess not buffed off in time, high humidity, or contamination on the surface. Solvent-based sealers flash differently in cold and humid conditions. Reheat the product, ensure surface is above 10°C, apply in thin coats, and buff off excess within 5 minutes.

> Cold weather — the solvent thickens and the product can't penetrate. It sits on top and dries as a haze. Always check product and surface temp.

## The Worx+ Process: Grouting with Totally Restore

The grouting step uses GRT double segments (120/140/160 grit) simultaneously grinding and filling. Wet the floor in front without puddles, apply Totally Restore and broom out for uniform thin coverage. Push the grinder through at a steady, slow pace while product is wet. > Broom out Totally Restore leaving uniform coverage without puddles. Push the grinder through at a steady slow pace while wet to grout the floor.

## The Worx+ Process: Potassium to Lithium Transition

A critical but often missed step in the process is knowing when to switch densifier types. Potassium silicate is used from grouting stage through to 200 grit resins — it works because the floor still has open pores it can penetrate. After 200 grit, the floor is too closed for potassium. Switch to Totally Boost Lithium at this point — its smaller molecule reaches the remaining micro-pores. Applying potassium after 200 grit is wasted product.

> Apply a lick coat of Lithium using a microfibre at the 200 grit stage. The floor is too closed for potassium now — lithium finds the pores potassium can no longer reach.

Source: Worx+ Polishing Process Guide · Official Technical Document


## The Worx+ Process: Totally Defend Application Protocol

After achieving target gloss, clean the floor with a dry microfibre mop. Shake Totally Defend well before opening. Warm cold product in the sun or a bucket of warm water before use — solvents separate from solids in cool climates. Apply 1–2 very light coats via microfibre mop. Once dry (approx. 30–40 min) buff the entire floor with a white or blue pad using a burnisher or polivac. Light application is critical — too much sealer left on the surface will not cure correctly.

> Ensure it is a very light application. Once dry (approx. 30–40 mins) buff the entire floor with a white or blue buffing pad.

Source: Worx+ Polishing Process Guide · Official Technical Document


## The Worx+ Process: Wet vs Dry Resin Stages

Each resin stage has a specific moisture requirement. #50 Specialty: fine mist of Totally Boost Potassium 5–10m ahead, run wet. #100 Specialty: even finer mist, run wet. #200 Specialty: run completely dry — no water at all. #400+ Universal/Flex: all dry. This progressively drier sequence matches the increasing density of the floor and prevents glazing, heat buildup, and tool damage.

> Run the 200 specialty completely dry. No misting. The floor is dense enough that water would hinder rather than help.

## Totally Defend: The Impregnating Sealer Explained

Totally Defend is a solvent-based impregnating sealer that penetrates concrete and chemically changes its surface tension — lining capillaries and voids to resist water and stain ingress. It is NOT a coating — it does not sit on top and cannot be scraped off. Apply in cool, dry conditions above 10°C surface temperature. Thin coats, buff off excess within the application window.

> It lines the capillaries and voids to create a state that gives increased resilience against water, stains and ingress. It is designed to be absorbed, not to sit on top.
